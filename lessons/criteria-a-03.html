A1 · Design need

Explain the problem in a real setting, with evidence, and justify why it needs a solution.

When a person is trapped in rubble, in a dark car park or in a loud stadium, they may only be able to shout or knock. Rescuers have to work out where a faint sound is coming from, and the ways they do this today have problems, including:

  1. Echoes give a false direction: In hard, empty spaces a sound bounces off walls, so people point to the wrong place.
  2. Background noise hides weak sounds: Machines, wind and crowds are louder than a trapped person's voice.
  3. People cannot judge direction well: In dark or enclosed spaces, a person listening has no way to measure where a sound comes from.
  4. Searching is slow: Every area has to be checked in turn, and a short call can be missed before the team arrives.
  5. No clear signal when a sound is found: Rescuers cannot tell the rest of the team that someone has heard something without shouting back.

Trapped people can often only make noise to attract attention, and finding where it comes from can decide where rescuers dig or search first (add a real source and the date you accessed it).

Why a solution is needed: Working out the direction of a sound quickly could help rescuers decide where to search first and reduce the time before a person is found.

A3 · Existing products

Study a range of similar products. For each: what it is, what works, what does not, and what you will take from it.

1. Smart speaker with a microphone array (such as Amazon Echo or Google Nest)

A home speaker with several microphones that works out where a voice is coming from.

Works well
Compares timing across microphones to pick out one voice.
Limits
Designed for quiet rooms, not rubble or noise.
What I will take from it
Comparing when a sound reaches several microphones.

2. Acoustic and seismic victim locators

Listening equipment used in search and rescue to pick up tapping and voices through rubble.

Works well
Very sensitive; used by professional teams.
Limits
Expensive and needs trained operators.
What I will take from it
Listening in noise and using several sensors.

3. Fish-finder sonar

Sends sound pulses into water and measures the echo to show what is below.

Works well
Measures distance and direction using sound.
Limits
Works in water, and gives echoes rather than finding a person's voice.
What I will take from it
Using timing to measure distance.

4. Speaker-tracking video-conference camera

A meeting-room camera that turns to face whoever is talking.

Works well
Turns toward a voice within moments.
Limits
Works in quiet rooms with one speaker at a time.
What I will take from it
Turning toward a sound automatically.

What this analysis tells me

These products come from very different fields, and none is designed for this client's exact setting. Comparing them shows many different ways of dealing with a similar problem. From them I will take:

  • Smart speaker with a microphone array: Comparing when a sound reaches several microphones.
  • Acoustic and seismic victim locators: Listening in noise and using several sensors.
  • Fish-finder sonar: Using timing to measure distance.
  • Speaker-tracking video-conference camera: Turning toward a sound automatically.

These ideas become the starting point for the three design concepts I sketch in B2.

A4 · Design brief

Summarise the problem, the client and your research. Cite the research codes and the B1 numbers in brackets, like (Ab and 7.1).

Rescue teams and facilities staff who must find a trapped person who cannot be seen, in a damaged building, an underground car park or a crowded stadium in the UAE, face a real problem. When a person is trapped and cannot be seen, rescuers depend on sound. Echoes and background noise make it hard to know which direction a weak sound comes from, and searching every area in turn is slow. (taken from my A1 problem analysis). Working out the direction of a sound quickly could help rescuers decide where to search first and reduce the time before a person is found.

My design brief is to develop a solution that helps rescue teams and facilities staff deal with this problem. From my research I found that A clap could be told apart from background noise up to about 3 m in a quiet corridor (EXEMPLAR DATA) (Aa and 5.1, 7.1). I also found that two listening points 60 mm or more apart gave a clear difference in timing (EXEMPLAR DATA) (Ad and 7.2, 6.1). After studying a wide range of existing products I learned that smart speakers and rescue listening devices compare the time a sound reaches several microphones (secondary research) (Ab and 8.1, 7.2). My research shows that the solution needs to do the following. It must turn to face a clap or voice within 2 seconds from up to 3 m away. (Ac and 7.1) It must compare the arrival time of a sound at points at least 60 mm apart. (Ad and 7.2) It must have no exposed moving parts that could pinch a hand. (Af and 6.1) It also has to be affordable and practical. It must cost no more than AED 70 in added parts. (Ae and 2.1) It must be possible to build and test in one 70-minute lesson. (Ae and 3.2) In my survey most responses preferred this colour scheme: matte black (Ag and 1.1), so I will use it.

The point of the design is to help rescue teams and facilities staff and keep people safer. It is connected with the global context “Scientific and technical innovation” because it responds to a real need in the community. It shows the Key Concept “Systems” because the parts of a solution have to work together as one system, and the Related Concepts “Function” and “Perspective” because the solution will be judged by how well it works for the people who use it. (Check these against your own unit planner.)
